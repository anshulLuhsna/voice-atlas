Suppose a Murmur listener says “Do **not** delete my bookmark.” One decoder drops the word *not*. A second decoder confuses *bookmark* with *book mark*. A downstream text normalizer could repair the second error. A system that immediately acts on the first one may destroy the listener’s place. Start an evaluation with the decision the score will support. In this case you need to know both how often a transcript changes intent and whether the product catches it before acting.

## Count errors with an explicit denominator

Align reference and hypothesis words in order, permitting a substitution, deletion or insertion at unit cost. The smallest total number of edits is their Levenshtein distance. With counts S, D and I from a minimum-cost alignment and N reference words, **WER = (S + D + I) / N**. Dropping *not* from the five-word reference above gives 1/5 = 20%. An insertion adds to the numerator without expanding the denominator, so WER can exceed 100%. Handle silent references separately by reporting hallucinated words or false speech events per minute of silence. Document the aligner’s treatment of optional words and normalization. [NIST scoring documentation](#source/sy-sctk)

Consider two clips with 1 error in 5 words and 3 in 95. Pooled WER is 4/100 = 4%. An unweighted mean over the two clip WERs gives about 11.6%. One estimate describes the error rate per reference word, the other the average clip. Report both if the product cares about short commands as well as passages. Define number expansion, case, punctuation, script transliteration and tokenization **before** scoring. For character error rate, state whether the unit is a Unicode code point or a grapheme cluster. Score punctuation and named entities separately if sentence boundaries and proper names drive retrieval. For a streaming decoder, log time to first stable word and count revisions to words already displayed.

## Ask ears different questions

For intelligibility, have listeners transcribe an unfamiliar clip, then score the transcription against intended text. For naturalness, present balanced clips in random order at comparable playback levels and ask for one defined rating. Average the ratings to obtain a mean opinion score (MOS), and publish the scale, anchors, listener count and dispersion. A change from 3.5 to 3.8 inherits the instructions, clips, devices and population of that study. For pronunciation, ask target-language listeners whether a name or contrast was produced acceptably, retaining regional variants. For speaker similarity, compare with a consented reference under a separate identity judgment. An embedding cosine score also responds to channel and recording conditions. [ITU-T P.808](#source/sy-p808)

Dramatization needs a longer trial. Blind listeners to model identity, counterbalance A/B order and permit a tie. Ask which version made it easier to follow a change of speaker. In another trial, replay the same character’s line from the beginning and end of a chapter and rate continuity. Balance text and voices across conditions, randomize the ordering for each listener, and record listening fatigue. A very clear sentence can still belong to the wrong character. Each question measures a distinct outcome.

## Build the set around listeners and decisions

Write a sampling frame with languages, dialects, self-described accents, code-switch points, devices, acoustic conditions, speaking styles and tasks. Recruit with consent and record missing cells. Label whose turn it was, intended words, source evidence, requested tool arguments and acceptable outcomes. On ambiguous boundaries collect independent labels, adjudicate with a documented rule and preserve disagreement. Keep speakers, sessions and near-duplicate passages on one side of a split. Reserve a frozen final test set and version the normalizer, model, fixture checksums and random seed.

Koenecke and colleagues observed recognition disparities across speaker groups in their US interview corpus. Their findings motivate sliced audits of a target population. For Indian languages, IndicFDB extends full-duplex evaluation across ten languages, but its timing heuristics and translation-based quality judgments introduce additional error pathways. Retain native-listener adjudication for disputed examples and report the number of speakers represented in each slice. [US ASR audit](#source/sy-disparities) · [IndicFDB](#source/sy-indicfdb)

## Compare systems under uncertainty

Run A and B on the same requests. Resample independent speakers with replacement, taking all of each sampled speaker’s trials in both systems. Recompute pooled WER for each version and store the paired difference. Across many resamples, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles provide a percentile bootstrap interval under these sampling assumptions. A dozen clips from one speaker do little to establish speaker diversity. In crossed listening studies where each listener hears several passages, account for both listener and item variation, for example with crossed random effects. Small slices deserve raw counts and a clear statement of uncertainty. [Statistical testing guide](#source/sy-stats)

For each question in Murmur, score evidence support, correct attribution to the author, tool and arguments, first audible answer and resumed source span. Define a time origin, e.g. the final human-annotated user speech sample. Summarize client-observed delays with median, 95th percentile, sample count and failures separately. A timeout that vanishes from the data makes the tail look reassuring. A text judge can triage unsupported claims. Give it a fixed rubric, blind the labels, swap order and compare against bilingual human judgments. Use listening for timing, prosody and fatigue. Text judges can favor answer position or verbosity. ## Check your reasoning

**Can excellent speaker similarity compensate for a mispronounced surname?** They answer different questions. Keep a name-specific acceptance criterion and a separate consented identity comparison. Inspect the individual trials behind a high average.

**A challenger wins on 60 of 100 clips from one narrator. Is this evidence that it wins across narrators?** It describes that narrator on those passages. Recruit additional narrators, retain paired conditions and estimate variation across the people to whom the claim should generalize.
