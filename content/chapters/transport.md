Imagine calling a museum guide. The guide finishes speaking and you begin a question. Half a second later, your headphones are still playing yesterday's decision to keep talking. Every component might be reporting success. The capture callback, network socket, decoder and audio output each successfully moved bytes. Their reports say little about when those bytes became useful.

## Follow one frame

A microphone produces samples on the capture device's clock. The software collects a frame, processes echo and noise, encodes it, attaches timing information and transmits it. The receiver reorders packets inside a playout buffer, conceals gaps it cannot repair before deadline, decodes to samples and hands those samples to an output device. A voice agent inserts feature extraction, recognition, decision making and synthesis between the two legs. Some stages work on successive chunks concurrently. Keep one monotonic clock for intervals on each machine. Comparing bare client and server timestamps treats their clock offset as network delay.

Start with 20 ms of mono PCM captured at 48,000 samples/s. The frame contains 960 samples. A signed 16-bit representation uses 1,920 bytes before headers or compression. Smaller packets reduce the wait to fill a packet but increase header overhead and scheduling work. A recognizer expecting 16 kHz needs a low-pass filter and resampling to 320 samples per 20 ms. Relabeling 48 kHz samples as 16 kHz stretches time threefold. Keep a mapping from resampled indices to capture time, especially when aligning a word to a playback event.

A sequence number reveals a missing or reordered packet. A media timestamp places its content on a timeline. An RTP timestamp has a codec-specific clock and an arbitrary initial offset. Opus uses a 48 kHz RTP clock even when its encoded audio bandwidth differs. Two consecutive 20 ms Opus packets normally start 960 timestamp ticks apart. Arrival times might differ by 27 ms. The 7 ms deviation is a sample of arrival variation. RTP's interarrival-jitter statistic smooths such differences over time, so packet jitter and receiver buffer depth measure different things. ## Choose a delivery contract

**Interactive browser conversation.** WebRTC negotiates codecs and routes, including relayed paths when direct connectivity fails. Its media stack uses protected RTP and RTCP feedback. The receiver can combine a jitter buffer, loss concealment and, where negotiated and timely, redundancy or retransmission. Target playout is a deadline. An overdue packet can be less useful than estimating its missing sound and continuing. Measure receiver loss, concealed samples and delta jitter-buffer delay divided by delta emitted count over the same interval. A longer buffer may lower concealment while making interruption sluggish. [WebRTC media transport](#source/rt-webrtc) · [Receiver statistics](#source/rt-stats)

**Audio over an application socket.** A WebSocket frames messages over an ordered reliable stream. Application code still chooses framing, codec, clocks, queues and cancellation. A missing TCP segment can delay newer audio already at the receiver. Reliability suits document narration, especially prefetch on a separate stream. Live capture requires a queue measured in milliseconds and a policy for obsolete audio. On overload, reject new work, shed expired live frames with a discontinuity marker, or slow background synthesis. If missing commands are unacceptable, keep them idempotent and acknowledged. [WebSocket framing and transport](#source/rt-websocket)

**Calling a telephone number.** A SIP INVITE offers to establish a session. Signaling negotiates how peers exchange media, commonly RTP through a gateway. A successful dialog does not prove that bidirectional audio flows. Inspect the negotiated codec, clock, channels and media addresses. A gateway can deliver 8 kHz μ-law audio to an application over its own WebSocket protocol. Decode μ-law first, then resample if the model demands it. Upsampling interpolates samples but cannot restore bandwidth lost on the call. [SIP session setup](#source/rt-sip) · [Example gateway media format](#source/rt-twilio)

## Keep playback out of capture

On speakerphone, the microphone records the far-end narrator along with the near-end listener. An echo canceller estimates a delayed, filtered version of the far-end signal using the playback reference and subtracts it from capture. The acoustic path changes when the phone moves. During double talk the canceller must preserve the listener who speaks over narration. Muting capture whenever output is active removes the very signal required for barge-in. Test with headphones and a speaker, at different gains. Check whether the echo reference includes the exact mix being played. [WebRTC audio processing requirements](#source/rt-audio)

## Work the queue

Suppose a server generates 60 ms of audio per 40 ms of wall time while a player consumes 40 ms. Without a bound the queue gains 20 ms every 40 ms. In one second it grows by 500 ms. Give playback a high-water mark, pause the producer above it and resume below a lower mark. Keep independent per-session limits. One slow listener must not block every other stream. After a reconnect, issue a new stream epoch, agree on the last confirmed playback checkpoint, reject prior-epoch frames and deduplicate command IDs. Retrying a whole audio buffer risks playing the same passage twice.

**Exercise.** In the [latency lab](#lab/latency), hold inference steady and add delay to a non-overlapping stage. Predict the change in first-audible latency, then compare it with the trace. On paper add a 120 ms output cushion to a design that predicts its reply 60 ms earlier. The net change in a serial path is 60 ms later. Overlapping work requires a critical-path calculation, which follows the last dependency to finish.

## Check your reasoning

**A trace says the server sent audio 80 ms ago. Is the listener hearing it?** The send event supplies a lower bound on remaining work. Transit, decode, buffered playback and the device still separate that event from presentation. Instrument the receiver and validate its estimate with a loopback recording.

**Why can stronger packet recovery make a conversation worse?** Waiting can save a syllable yet make the entire turn late. Compare late loss and turn timing together, including tail latency. An archival recording has a different tradeoff because its replay can wait.
